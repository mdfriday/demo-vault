# First Customer

The first customer is a rite of passage because money converts a hobby narrative into a responsibility narrative.

Before the first payment, you can tell yourself the product is "almost ready." After the first payment, someone else has a stake in your promises. That is the beginning of a real company — even if the company is still just you, a vault, and a laptop.

## How it happened

A real conversation. A clear pain around publishing notes. A demo that respected their vault. A price said out loud without apology.

It was closer to [[Learning Sales]] than to "growth." There was no clever funnel — there was a person who had already tried heavier DIY paths and wanted a calmer bridge from Obsidian to a public site. They did not need me to invent a new philosophy of writing. They needed publish to stop being a weekend project.

## What I felt

Relief, then fear. Relief that the problem was real enough to buy. Fear that I now owed reliability. That fear is useful if it drives craft; poisonous if it drives endless postponement of the next conversation.

I also felt a strange quiet. No confetti. Just a notification and a new kind of seriousness.

## What changed in me

I stopped polishing in the dark. I started asking what would make the next customer succeed without me sitting beside them. Docs, clearer publish UX, and sharper positioning around [[MDFriday Publish]] mattered more than another decorative landing section.

I wrote objections down immediately. Those notes later became patterns in [[First 10 Paying Customers]] and checklists in [[How to Find Your First Customers]].

## What I would tell past me

Charge sooner than your ego wants. Demo with their materials when possible. Do not confuse compliment-energy with purchase-energy. Keep the promise narrow — [[Why Focus on Publish]] — so you can actually keep it. And celebrate briefly, then return to the work.

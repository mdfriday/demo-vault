# Flip Features into Benefits: The So What Test

## What it is

"Features tell, but benefits sell." The course draws a simple line:

- A **feature** is *what the product can do* (or has).
- A **benefit** is *what you can do with the product*: the result the customer gets.

To create desire you have to sell the result, not only the product. The tool for flipping one into the other is the **"So what?" test**: take each feature, ask "so what?" from the customer's point of view, and keep going until you reach a result they care about.

The course's main example is Apple's iPod launch. Apple didn't lead with the feature "1 GB of storage for MP3s", which most people couldn't picture. It led with the benefit **"1,000 songs in your pocket"**. That was instantly clear, and it also made the advantage over alternatives obvious. The course contrasts it with a MiniDisc or a CD, which held only a few dozen songs.

More course examples:

| Feature | So what? Benefit |
| --- | --- |
| Shampoo contains a special formula (panthenol) | Beautiful hair that gets you noticed |
| Bag has a rain cover | Keeps your gear dry even in torrential rain |
| Fitness program includes a meal plan and shaker | You'll know exactly what to eat |

## Why it matters

Customers don't buy specs. They buy the change in their situation. Feature language asks the reader to do the translation themselves, and most won't. Benefit language does the translation for them. It powers the **desire** stage of [[aida-formula|AIDA]], makes headlines more compelling, and helps customers compare you with alternatives the way the iPod example did. Use it on landing pages, product descriptions, launch posts, emails and demos. Technical founders especially should use it, since they tend to describe what they built rather than what it lets people do.

## How to use it

**Inputs:** a full feature list, and customer pain points and desires in their words ([[customer-pain-points-and-desires|Pain Points and Desires]]). **Output:** a feature → benefit table and benefit-led copy.

1. **List every feature** in plain words, including technical ones.
2. **Ask "so what?"** for each one, as the customer. Write the answer. Ask again if the answer is still about the product ("it's fast" → so what? → "your site is live before you've finished your coffee").
3. **Stop at a result the customer would say out loud,** ideally matching language from your research.
4. **Make it concrete.** Like "1,000 songs", make the benefit something people can picture or measure, but only with claims you can support.
5. **Add the comparison** where it helps: what is better compared with what they use now?
6. **Rank the benefits** by how closely they match the top pain points. Lead with the strongest in your headline and first bullets.
7. **Write copy as "benefit, because feature":** the benefit first, the feature as proof ("Keep your gear dry in torrential rain, thanks to the built-in rain cover").

> [!example] Illustration: MDFriday
> Feature: "Right-click a folder in Obsidian to build a static site." So what? You don't leave Obsidian. So what? You can publish your notes without learning a new tool or restructuring your vault. Benefit line: "Publish a folder of notes as a website without leaving Obsidian." Feature: "Multiple themes for the same note." So what? The same notes can become a blog, docs or a portfolio. Illustrative. Confirm the benefits with real users.

## How to verify it worked

- **Five-second test:** show the page or post to someone in your audience for a few seconds, then ask what they'd get from the product. If they repeat a benefit, it worked. If they repeat a spec, or can't answer, it didn't.
- **A/B test:** compare a benefit-led headline or bullet list against a feature-led one on the same channel. Track click, sign-up or install rates.
- **Customer echo:** in replies, reviews and interviews, do customers describe the product using your benefit phrases? That shows the framing matches how they see it.
- **Question patterns:** many "but what does it do for me?" questions point to feature-heavy copy.

**Good:** readers can state the outcome in their own words. **Bad:** readers understand how it works but not why they'd want it.

## How to keep optimizing

- **Re-run the test when you add features.** Every new feature needs a benefit before it's announced.
- **Collect benefits from customers.** Ask existing users what changed for them, and replace your guessed benefits with their real ones.
- **Test benefit order.** Try different leading benefits in headlines and see which gets the best response.
- **Refine concreteness.** Swap abstract benefits ("save time") for specific, supportable ones ("publish in one step").
- **Segment.** Different audiences value different benefits of the same feature. Adjust per channel.

## Common mistakes

- Stopping at a half-benefit that is still about the product ("fast", "powerful").
- Leading with technical specs your audience can't picture.
- Inventing benefits you can't deliver or measure.
- Dropping features entirely. They still matter as proof behind the benefit.
- Using the same benefit for every audience.

## Checklist

- [ ] Full feature list written
- [ ] "So what?" asked until each reaches a customer result
- [ ] Benefits concrete and supportable
- [ ] Comparison with alternatives where useful
- [ ] Copy leads with the benefit, feature as proof
- [ ] Five-second test or A/B test run

# Research First: The Digital Marketing Build Order

## What it is

Digital marketing is not a pile of channels you try at random. The course frames it as a **sequence**, where each step depends on the one before:

1. **Market research**: learn what people struggle with, what they want, and the words they use for both.
2. **A home base**: a website (and, soon after, an email list) that you own, where every channel can send people.
3. **Traffic and sales**: channels such as SEO, YouTube, social platforms, Q&A sites and paid ads, which bring people to the home base and turn them into leads and customers.

The course calls this order "logical" and recommends doing the assignments in sequence if you are building a business from scratch. It also says each channel section works on its own. If you already have a business and only want to grow one channel, you can jump straight to it.

The core idea is simple. **Channels amplify a message. They cannot create one.** If you don't know the customer's pain points and desires, more traffic only shows your weak message to more people.

## Why it matters

Reach for this framework whenever you feel pulled to "just start posting" or "just run some ads". The course's market research lectures give the failure pattern behind it: people complain that their ads didn't work, and when you look at the ads, they don't address a single pain point or desire. The problem was never the channel. The step before it was skipped.

The sequence is also a way to decide what to work on this week. If your research is weak, work on research. If you have a clear message but nowhere to send people, work on the home base. Channel work only makes sense once the first two are in place.

## How to use it

**Inputs:** your product idea or product, a rough idea of who it is for, and an honest look at what you already have.

**Procedure:**

1. **Audit each stage.** Write three lines: what you know about customer pain points and desires (with evidence), what your home base is (site, landing page, list), and which channels you use today.
2. **Find the lowest unfinished stage.** Research counts as done when you can name the top problems in your customers' own words and say which ones are poorly solved today. See [[customer-pain-points-and-desires|Pain Points and Desires]]. The home base counts as done when a visitor can understand the offer and take one clear action (see [[call-to-action|Call to Action]]).
3. **Work only on that stage** until it meets its "done" bar. Resist working on stages further down.
4. **Pick one channel first.** When you reach the traffic stage, choose the channel where your research showed customers already gather. Treat each channel as its own project with its own assignment, as the course does.
5. **Loop back.** What you learn from the channels (comments, replies, objections) goes back into research.

**Output:** a short written plan that says which stage you're on, what "done" means for it, and the next action.

> [!example] Illustration: a solo founder building MDFriday
> Say the goal is the first 10 international users of an Obsidian publishing plugin. Research: collect how Obsidian users publish today and what frustrates them, for example with the [[three-question-market-research-survey|three-question survey]] and Reddit threads. Home base: a landing page that states the main benefit in the users' own words, with one action (install the plugin). Traffic: start with the one channel where the research found these users talking (for this stage, Reddit), then add YouTube for trust. This is an illustration of the framework, not a claim about current MDFriday results.

## How to verify it worked

You are not measuring the framework itself. You are checking whether each stage is really finished before you move on:

- **Research stage:** can you list the top three to five problems with direct quotes and a count of how often each came up? Can you say which ones people rate "very difficult" to solve? If you can't quote customers, the stage isn't done.
- **Home base stage:** show the page to a few target users and ask, "What is this, who is it for, and what would you do next?" If they can't answer in one sentence, the message isn't clear yet. Once it has traffic, track the share of visitors who take the main action. Compare that number over time, not against someone else's benchmark.
- **Traffic stage:** for each channel, track people who reach the home base and people who take the main action. A channel that brings visits but no actions usually points to a message or home-base problem, not a channel problem.

**Good signs:** each channel reuses the same research-backed message, and the customer's language shows up in your headlines. **Bad signs:** you're changing channels every week, or you can't say which stage is blocking you.

## How to keep optimizing

- **Run a monthly stage review.** Re-audit the three stages. New research findings often mean rewriting the home-base message before you touch any channel.
- **Add one channel at a time.** Only add a second channel when the first one delivers people in a way you can repeat.
- **Feed channel data back into research.** Questions and objections in comments are free market research. Log them in the same place as your survey answers.
- **Re-run the research periodically.** Pain points change as the market changes. Run the survey again whenever results in your channels stall.

## Common mistakes

- Starting with ads or social posts before you know the customer's problems.
- Treating a failing channel as a channel problem when the message is the problem.
- Running five channels at once and doing all of them badly.
- Building a site that sends visitors nowhere: no list, no clear action.
- Treating research as a one-time task instead of a loop.

## Checklist

- [ ] I can name my customers' top problems in their words, with evidence.
- [ ] I know which problems are poorly solved today.
- [ ] My home base has one clear main action.
- [ ] I'm focused on one channel, chosen from my research.
- [ ] Insights from channels go back into my research notes.

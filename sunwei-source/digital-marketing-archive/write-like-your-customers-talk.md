# Write Like Your Customers Talk

## What it is

The course calls it the one rule to master in copywriting: **write like your customers talk.** You take the pain points and desires your prospects describe, in their own words, and put them into your sales pages, emails, blog posts and ads.

It rests on the course's definition of copywriting: *rearranging words to make things sound better*, or **salesmanship in text form**. A good salesperson listens first and then repeats the customer's problem back better than the customer could. Copy that uses the customer's own language does the same thing on the page. When prospects read it, the course says, they think: "this product is exactly for me, this is different to other solutions I've seen, and I really want this."

This practice is often called using the **voice of the customer**. The course's market research intro sets it up: the same research that finds what to build also collects "powerful sales copy" for your pages, ads and emails.

## Why it matters

- **Recognition beats persuasion.** People trust copy that describes their situation in the words they would use.
- **It removes guesswork.** You don't have to invent clever lines. The best lines are often already in your survey answers.
- **It sharpens every other copy technique.** Headlines, AIDA's interest and desire sections, benefit bullets and CTAs all work better with customer language.

Use it whenever you write anything meant to persuade: a landing page, a launch email, a Reddit post, an ad.

## How to use it

**Inputs:** a bank of verbatim customer quotes from any of the [[four-customer-research-methods|four research methods]], especially survey Questions 1 and 2. **Output:** copy where the key phrases come from real customers.

1. **Build a quote bank.** Copy exact phrases, typos and slang included, into a document, grouped into *pain*, *desire*, *objection* and *past attempts*.
2. **Highlight the vivid phrases:** concrete, emotional or surprising wording ("I spend my Sundays fighting with config files").
3. **Map quotes to copy slots:**
   - **Headline:** the most common pain or desire, in their words ([[magnetic-headlines|Magnetic Headlines]]).
   - **First paragraph:** name the situation the way they describe it, to hold interest ([[aida-formula|AIDA]]).
   - **Benefit bullets:** desires from Question 2 ("so I can finally...") paired with features ([[features-into-benefits|Flip Features into Benefits]]).
   - **Objection handling:** past attempts and doubts, answered directly.
   - **CTA:** the outcome they asked for ([[call-to-action|Call to Action]]).
4. **Write the draft,** changing customer phrases only as much as grammar and clarity need.
5. **Edit out insider language.** Remove your internal jargon and technical terms the customer never used.
6. **Read it to a customer** or post it where they gather, and watch the reaction.

> [!example] Illustration: MDFriday
> Say several users wrote something like "I just want my notes online without learning Hugo." A headline could be "Publish your Obsidian notes as a website, without learning Hugo." That uses their words rather than "a zero-config static publishing pipeline". The quote here is illustrative. Use real ones from your own research.

## How to verify it worked

- **Recognition signals:** replies like "this is exactly my problem", people quoting your line back, or DMs describing the same situation.
- **A/B comparison:** test a customer-language headline or email subject against a version in your own wording, on the same channel and audience. Compare click, open or reply rates.
- **Scroll or read depth:** where your tools allow, check whether more readers get past the first paragraph after you rewrite it in customer language.
- **Conversion to the main action:** sign-ups or installs per visitor before and after the rewrite. Change one element at a time so you know what caused the difference.
- **Jargon count:** a quick self-check. How many terms on the page would a new customer need explained?

**Good:** people say it feels like it was written for them. **Bad:** readers ask "what does this actually do?", which usually means insider language crept back in.

## How to keep optimizing

- **Keep the quote bank alive.** Add phrases from every comment, support email and interview.
- **Rotate the language.** Swap in new phrases for headlines and subject lines, and keep the winners.
- **Segment your language.** Different audiences describe the same pain differently. Keep separate phrase lists by segment and channel.
- **Watch objections.** New objections in comments tell you what to add to the page.
- **Re-research when results fade.** Language goes stale as markets change. Refresh it with a new survey or listening session.

## Common mistakes

- Writing from your own product vocabulary instead of the customer's.
- "Cleaning up" quotes until the emotion is gone.
- Using customer language in the headline but falling back to feature lists in the body.
- Mixing phrases from very different audiences on one page.
- Collecting quotes once and never refreshing them.

## Checklist

- [ ] Quote bank exists, grouped by pain / desire / objection
- [ ] Headline uses a real customer phrase
- [ ] First paragraph describes the situation as customers do
- [ ] Bullets pair features with desires in customer words
- [ ] Internal jargon removed
- [ ] Customer-language version tested against the old version

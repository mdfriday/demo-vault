# 001. Why We Switched to Storage-Based Pricing

*Sep 21–27, 2026*

**Current stage goal**: Find the first 10 international users who genuinely need MDFriday.

## What I expected

At the beginning of the week, I thought the next step for MDFriday was straightforward:

Ship more features.

I expected users to compare publishing solutions based on capabilities, so I spent most of my attention on expanding what MDFriday could do.

My assumption was simple:

> More features → More value → More users.

## What actually happened

This week I shipped two new Digital Garden themes:

- Quartz (Classic)
- Flint (Simple)

Together with Jasper, MDFriday now offers three Digital Garden themes for Obsidian users.

At the same time, I reworked the free plan.

The previous model was based on site limits and periodic resets. Technically it worked, but it was surprisingly difficult to explain.

Users had questions like:

- What counts as a site?
- Why is there a limit?
- What happens when it resets?
- Can I safely publish multiple projects?

The more I explained it, the more complicated it sounded.

So I simplified it.

The new free plan is:

- Unlimited sites
- 50 MB storage
- No monthly reset
- Storage archived only after long-term inactivity

Instead of limiting how users organize their content, the plan is now centered around storage.

Much easier to understand.

Much easier to trust.

I also continued talking with users in Reddit, Obsidian communities, and WeChat groups.

A pattern started appearing repeatedly:

People were rarely asking for more features.

Instead, they were asking questions like:

- How quickly can I publish?
- Do I need to change my existing Markdown?
- Can I use my own domain?
- Can I leave the platform later?

The conversation was much more about simplicity and ownership than functionality.

## What I learned

This week reinforced two ideas.

### 1. Users buy confidence, not features

Most experienced Obsidian users already know there are many ways to publish notes online.

The problem is rarely "Is publishing possible?"

The real question is:

> Is publishing simple enough that I'll actually do it?

A feature only creates value if users can understand it, trust it, and use it without friction.

Adding capabilities is useful.

Reducing uncertainty is often more useful.

### 2. Every step reduces conversion

While looking at the publishing flow, I realized that every additional action creates another opportunity for users to leave.

One example was Guest Publishing.

Previously, guest users had to complete a human verification step before publishing.

From a system perspective, this made sense.

From a user perspective, it was friction.

Someone who wants to test publishing should be able to move from:

Plugin Installed → Publish

with as few interruptions as possible.

So we removed the verification step from the guest flow and moved abuse prevention to the server side instead.

The lesson was simple:

> Every step must justify its existence.

If a step does not directly help the user achieve their goal, it is probably hurting activation.

## What changed my mind

I used to think limits were a good way to define a free plan.

Now I think clarity is more important than limits.

Users rarely think in terms of:

- How many sites can I create?

They think in terms of:

- Can I publish this note?
- Can I publish this folder?
- Can I experiment without worrying?

Storage is easier to understand than site count.

Ownership is easier to understand than restrictions.

I also used to think feature growth was the primary path to growth.

Now I'm increasingly convinced that reducing friction is just as important.

Sometimes removing a step creates more value than adding a feature.

## What I'm doing next

The next priority is not adding more themes.

The next priority is understanding the activation funnel:

Plugin Installed → First Publish → First Return Visit → Paid User

I want to identify where users stop and why.

The goal is simple:

Make the path from idea to published website as short as possible.

Because every unnecessary step is a chance to lose someone who was ready to try.

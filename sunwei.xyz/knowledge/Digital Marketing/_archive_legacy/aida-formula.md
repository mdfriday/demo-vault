# The AIDA Formula

## What it is

**AIDA** is a copywriting structure with four stages: **Attention, Interest, Desire, Action**. It is designed to grab a reader's attention and carry them through your content to the point where they act. The course recommends using it for almost everything: sales pages, blog posts, email newsletters, video scripts, Facebook ads, Google ads. It also says you "shouldn't be writing from scratch."

The four parts as the course teaches them:

1. **Attention:** won with the **headline, subject line or video title**. The reader is asking "what's in it for me? Why should I invest my time?" If they don't open, click or watch, nothing else matters.
2. **Interest:** turn attention into interest **in the first paragraph**, or you lose them. Engage with **unusual, counterintuitive or fresh information**. The course's example admits other tools exist, then says "I bet you haven't seen anything like this before."
3. **Desire:** make them want it. The course gives three ways:
   - show the **product benefits**
   - explain **how it changes their life or solves their problem**
   - show **results others have achieved**, such as testimonials or endorsements
4. **Action:** ask for the next step with a clear, visible call to action (buy, sign up, watch, share), ideally with an incentive and urgency. The course's example: early-adopter pricing available only for the next few days.

## Why it matters

AIDA gives you a checklist for the reader's mental journey. Most weak copy fails at a specific stage: a dull headline (attention), a slow opening (interest), a list of features with no outcomes (desire), or no clear ask (action). Knowing the stages lets you find and fix the broken one. Use AIDA as the default skeleton for any piece whose job is to get a response.

## How to use it

**Inputs:** your audience's pain points and desires (ideally in their words), the product's key benefits, any proof, and the single action you want. **Output:** a draft where each section does one AIDA job.

1. **Decide the action first.** Write down the one thing the reader should do at the end.
2. **Attention:** write 10 or more headline options using [[magnetic-headlines|headline techniques]] and pick the one that answers "what's in it for me?" most clearly.
3. **Interest:** open with something fresh: a surprising fact you can back up, a counterintuitive point, or the reader's own situation described vividly ([[write-like-your-customers-talk|in their words]]). Acknowledge the alternatives they know, then hint at why this is different.
4. **Desire:** add benefit-led bullets ([[features-into-benefits|flip features into benefits]]), a short before-and-after of their life, and proof: real testimonials, results or examples. Use only proof you actually have.
5. **Action:** a verb-led, visible CTA ([[call-to-action|Call to Action]]), with a genuine incentive or deadline if one exists ([[urgency-in-copy|Honest Urgency]]) and something that lowers the risk ([[risk-reversal|Risk Reversal]]).
6. **Review stage by stage.** Read only the headline: would you open it? Read only the first paragraph: would you keep reading? And so on.

> [!example] Illustration: an MDFriday launch email
> **Attention** (subject): "Publish your Obsidian folder as a website in one right-click." **Interest**: "You've probably tried static site generators that want you to restructure your vault. This works the other way round: your notes stay as they are." **Desire**: benefits ("keep writing in Obsidian, your site updates from the same files") plus a real user quote, if one exists. **Action**: "Try it on one folder today," with a link. Illustrative copy only, and all claims must match the real product.

## How to verify it worked

Measure each stage with the metric closest to it, and compare versions against each other rather than against outside benchmarks (the course gives none):

| Stage | Signal | How to measure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Attention | Open or click rate | Email subject opens, click rate on titles and thumbnails |
| Interest | Continued reading | Scroll depth, time on page, video retention past the intro |
| Desire | Engagement with the offer | Clicks on pricing or details, replies asking questions |
| Action | Conversion | Sign-ups, installs or purchases per visitor or recipient |

A big drop at one stage tells you which section to rewrite. **Good:** a smooth funnel with no single stage losing most people. **Bad:** strong opens but almost no clicks, which usually means the body didn't create interest or desire.

## How to keep optimizing

- **Fix the weakest stage first,** and change one thing at a time.
- **Test headlines constantly.** Attention sets the ceiling for everything below it.
- **Refresh the interest hook** when your audience has seen it. Fresh information loses its freshness.
- **Collect proof as a habit.** Ask happy users for a quote. It strengthens the desire section.
- **Match AIDA to the channel.** A tweet may compress AIDA into three lines. A sales page expands each stage.

## Common mistakes

- Starting with the product instead of the reader's "what's in it for me?"
- Jumping from the headline straight to "buy now" without building interest and desire.
- Filling the desire section with features instead of outcomes and proof.
- Faking urgency or testimonials, which destroys trust.
- Ending without a CTA. The course says everything you write should have one.

## Checklist

- [ ] One action decided before writing
- [ ] Headline answers "what's in it for me?"
- [ ] First paragraph offers something fresh or counterintuitive
- [ ] Desire section has benefits, life change and real proof
- [ ] Clear, visible CTA with honest incentive or urgency
- [ ] Each stage measured, weakest stage fixed first

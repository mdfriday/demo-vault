# Four Ways to Uncover Pain Points and Desires

## What it is

The course names four practical ways to learn a customer's pain points and desires:

1. **Customer surveys**, structured questions sent to many people (see [[three-question-market-research-survey|the three-question survey]]).
2. **Social media and communities**, reading what people already say in Facebook groups, Reddit, Instagram, Twitter and similar places.
3. **Review sites and marketplaces**, reading the **five-star** reviews (what people love about existing products) and the **one-star** reviews (what they hate), to spot what is missing in the market.
4. **Customer interviews**, one-to-one calls (the course mentions Skype or phone) to dig into what holds people back and why current solutions don't work for them.

Each method gives a different kind of evidence. Together they tell you what people struggle with, why it matters, and where current options fall short. That last part is the raw material for a unique selling proposition.

## Why it matters

Surveys need an audience willing to answer. Communities and reviews let you listen without asking anyone for anything. Interviews give depth that no form can. Having all four means you're never stuck, even with zero followers. The four methods also support each other: a theme that shows up in Reddit threads, one-star reviews *and* interviews is far more reliable than one from a single source.

Reach for this toolkit at the start of any product or campaign, or whenever copy stops connecting and you need fresh customer language.

## How to use it

**Output for every method:** exact quotes, tagged *pain* or *desire*, stored in one shared log with the source.

**1. Surveys (breadth).** Use the three-question survey when you can reach a group of people. Best for counting how common each problem is and how hard it has been to solve.

**2. Social listening (unprompted truth).**
- Find where your audience talks: specific subreddits, forums, groups.
- Search for complaint and wish phrases alongside your topic, such as "frustrated", "is there a way to", "alternative to", "how do you".
- Copy exact wording and note how many people agree (replies, upvotes).
- Participate honestly. Answer questions instead of pitching.

**3. Review mining (the competitive gap).**
- Pick the main existing solutions (products, courses, plugins, books).
- Read five-star reviews to learn what outcome people value most.
- Read one-star reviews to learn what breaks, disappoints or is missing.
- Middle ratings often explain trade-offs ("great, but...").
- Write down recurring "missing" items. They are candidate differentiators.

**4. Interviews (depth and why).**
- Invite people who showed strong pain (for example survey respondents who said "very difficult").
- Ask about past behavior, not hypothetical futures: "Walk me through the last time you tried to...", "What did you try? What happened? Why didn't it work?"
- Keep asking "why does that matter?" to reach the real motivation.
- Record (with permission) or take verbatim notes.

**Synthesis.** Cluster all quotes into themes. For each theme, note which methods it appeared in, how often, and how hard people find it to solve.

> For an Obsidian publishing plugin: listen in Obsidian-related subreddits and the Obsidian forum for "how do I publish my vault" threads. Mine reviews and issue trackers of existing publishing options for recurring complaints. Then interview a few people who described the problem vividly. ## How to verify it worked

- **Triangulation:** your top themes appear in at least two different methods. A theme found in only one source is a hypothesis, not a finding.
- **Saturation:** new interviews or threads stop revealing new themes, and you keep hearing the same things. That suggests you've covered the main ground for this audience.
- **Quote bank size:** you have enough verbatim phrases to write a headline, a first paragraph and benefit bullets without inventing language.
- **Predictive power:** a message built from these findings gets a better response (replies, clicks, sign-ups) than your previous message on the same channel.

**Good:** clear, repeated themes with a known gap in existing solutions. **Bad:** a heap of notes with no clusters, or findings that only confirm what you already believed. That often means leading questions or a narrow source.

## How to keep optimizing

- **Make listening a habit** with a small fixed time each week, and log quotes as you go.
- **Rotate sources** so one community's culture doesn't skew your picture.
- **Improve interview questions** after each call. Drop questions that get generic answers.
- **Re-mine reviews** when competitors release updates. Their new one-star reviews show the next gap.
- **Close the loop.** When you act on an insight, tell the people who gave it. It builds trust and brings more feedback.

## Common mistakes

- Pitching in communities instead of listening, which gets you ignored or banned.
- Reading only competitors' negative reviews and missing what customers value most.
- Asking hypothetical interview questions ("Would you pay for...?") instead of asking about real past behavior.
- Keeping insights in scattered places so they never get clustered.
- Treating what you think the problem is as research.

## Checklist

- [ ] At least two of the four methods used for this topic
- [ ] Exact quotes logged in one place with the source
- [ ] Five-star and one-star reviews both read
- [ ] Interviews ask about past behavior and "why"
- [ ] Themes clustered and checked across methods
- [ ] Gaps in existing solutions written down

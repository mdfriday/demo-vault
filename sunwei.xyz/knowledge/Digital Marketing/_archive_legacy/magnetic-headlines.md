# Magnetic Headlines: Proven Headline Patterns

## What it is

A headline (or title, or subject line) is the **attention** stage of [[aida-formula|AIDA]]. It decides whether anything else gets read. The course quotes advertising pioneer David Ogilvy: on average **five times as many people read the headline as the body copy**, so once you've written your headline you have spent "80 cents of your dollar." The course's takeaway: put far more effort into the headline than its length suggests.

The course's pattern-based tips:

1. **Start with a number:** "21 actionable SEO techniques you can use right now." Numbers create curiosity, give you an easy list structure to write to, and make the piece easy to scan.
2. **Use "how to", especially with "even if" or "without":** "How to [do X] even if [common obstacle]" or "How to [do X] without [common objection]". This speaks to people who ran into problems existing solutions don't handle.
3. **Flag common mistakes:** "16 copywriting mistakes you're probably making (and how to fix them)." Readers want to check whether they make them.
4. **Ask a question:** "Think you know SEO? Quiz yourself and find out!"
5. **Be ultra specific:** the headline sets the expectation for the whole piece. "Write a song" is weak. "How to compose your first song: songwriting 101" is clear.
6. **Use the five Ws** (who, what, when, where, why) to signal what information you'll give: "These 7 questions will reveal why you're still single."
7. **Pair it with an image** that grabs attention and shows the essence of the piece visually.

(The course's tips on keyword placement and length are in [[search-friendly-titles|Search-Friendly Titles]]. Its tips on modeling experts and practice are in [[headline-variations-and-swipe-files|Headline Variations and Swipe Files]].)

## Why it matters

If the headline fails, the best body copy, product or offer never gets seen. Headlines also force clarity: if you can't write a specific headline, you probably don't know what the piece promises. Use these patterns for blog posts, videos, emails, social posts, landing pages and ads.

## How to use it

**Inputs:** the piece's single promise, the reader's pain or desire in their words ([[write-like-your-customers-talk|Write Like Your Customers Talk]]), and any real number the content supports. **Output:** one chosen headline, plus a few runners-up for testing.

1. **Write the promise in one plain sentence:** "This shows [who] how to [get outcome]."
2. **Draft at least one headline per pattern:** number, how-to with even if / without, mistakes, question, five Ws.
3. **Add specificity:** a real number, a concrete outcome, a named audience or a timeframe the content actually delivers.
4. **Check the expectation.** Does the body fully deliver what the headline promises? If not, change the headline, not the reader's trust.
5. **Pick the one that answers "what's in it for me?"** most quickly for your target reader.
6. **Choose or create a matching image** that explains the headline visually. (The course suggests free stock photo sites. Check the license of any image you use.)
7. **Keep two or three runners-up** to test later.

> [!example] Illustration: MDFriday
> Promise: Obsidian users can publish a folder as a website without extra tooling.
> - Number: "3 ways to publish your Obsidian vault (and the one that needs no setup)"
> - How-to + without: "How to turn an Obsidian folder into a website without learning a static site generator"
> - Mistakes: "5 mistakes that make publishing Obsidian notes painful"
> - Question: "Still copying notes into a CMS? There's a shorter path"
> Illustrative drafts. Each must match what the content really delivers.

## How to verify it worked

- **Click-through or open rate:** the share of people who saw the headline and clicked or opened. Compare headline versions on the same channel and audience (email split tests, or a post republished where the platform allows, or YouTube title tests if available).
- **Promise-delivery match:** time on page, scroll depth or video retention after the click. A headline that gets clicks followed by fast exits over-promised.
- **Qualitative signal:** comments show whether people understood what the piece was about.
- **Pattern log:** track which pattern each headline used and its result. Over time you'll see which patterns work for *your* audience.

**Good:** above your own usual click or open rate, with healthy reading after the click. **Bad:** high clicks with quick bounces (clickbait), or low clicks on strong content (a headline that's too vague or too clever).

## How to keep optimizing

- **Test one variable at a time,** for example number versus question with the same promise.
- **Keep a winners file** of your best-performing headlines and reuse their structure.
- **Refresh old content.** Re-title underperforming posts or videos and compare before and after.
- **Mine new customer phrases** for fresh headline language.
- **Retire overused patterns** if your audience starts ignoring them.

## Common mistakes

- Spending seconds on the headline and hours on the body.
- Vague or clever headlines that don't say what the reader gets.
- Headlines that promise more than the content delivers.
- Invented numbers ("10x your traffic") that the content can't support.
- An image that has nothing to do with the headline.

## Checklist

- [ ] One-sentence promise written first
- [ ] Drafts using several patterns
- [ ] Specific: number, outcome or audience
- [ ] Body fully delivers the promise
- [ ] Matching image with a valid license
- [ ] Result logged and compared with alternatives

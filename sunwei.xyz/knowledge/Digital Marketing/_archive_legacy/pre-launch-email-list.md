# Build an Email List Before You Have a Product

## What it is

A way to start an email list of likely customers **before** you have a product, a blog post or any content. You add one optional field at the end of a research survey:

> "Add your email below if you'd like to be contacted when we have a solution to your problem."

The course presents this as a bonus to the [[three-question-market-research-survey|three-question survey]]: the same process that finds problems and collects copy "will help you to build an email list of people to sell to even before you have a product or have ever written a blog post."

What makes it work: the list is **self-selected by pain**. Everyone on it has just described a problem and asked to hear when it's solved. That is a far warmer list than one collected with a generic "subscribe for updates".

## Why it matters

- **Launch day isn't silent.** When you ship, you have people to tell who asked to hear from you.
- **Demand signal.** The number of opt-ins, and who they are, tells you how much people want a solution.
- **Research panel.** The same people can be interviewed, sent beta builds, and asked for feedback.
- **Owned channel.** Unlike followers on a platform, the list is yours. This fits the home-base step in [[research-first-marketing-sequence|Research First]].

Use it any time you run research with people who might become customers.

## How to use it

**Inputs:** a research survey or conversation, and a place to store emails with the context they came from. **Output:** a segmented early list and a plan to contact it.

1. **Add the optional field** at the end of the survey, after the problem, outcome and difficulty questions. People are most willing right after describing their pain.
2. **Make the promise specific and honest:** you'll contact them when there's a solution to *their* problem. Don't imply you'll send a newsletter if you won't.
3. **Keep the link to their answers.** Store each email next to that person's problem theme and difficulty rating, so you can later write to "people who said X is very difficult" with a message about exactly X.
4. **Follow the relevant consent rules** for where your respondents live (for example, keep a record of what they agreed to and give an easy way to opt out). This is general good practice, not course content.
5. **Send a short thank-you** soon after, if your tool allows. Say what you learned and when you expect to be in touch. This keeps the list warm.
6. **Use the list during building.** Invite the "very difficult" segment to interviews or early access.
7. **On launch,** email each segment with copy built from their own words (see [[write-like-your-customers-talk|Write Like Your Customers Talk]]) and one clear [[call-to-action|call to action]].

> [!example] Illustration: MDFriday
> A survey of Obsidian users ends with "Add your email if you'd like to hear when we have a solution to publishing your vault as a site." Later, the people who described "links break when I export" get an email that opens with that exact problem and invites them to try a folder publish. This is a sketch of the method, not a record of a real campaign.

## How to verify it worked

- **Opt-in rate:** the share of respondents who leave an email. Compare it across audiences and survey versions. A higher rate in one audience suggests stronger need there. The course gives no benchmark, so judge by trend and comparison.
- **Segment quality:** what share of opt-ins rated their problem "very difficult"? A list heavy with low-difficulty problems is less valuable.
- **Engagement when contacted:** open and reply rates on your first emails. Replies are the strongest signal at this stage.
- **Conversion at launch:** how many list members try, install or buy compared with people from other sources.

**Good:** people reply to your emails with more details about their problem. **Bad:** a large list that doesn't open anything, which usually means too much time passed or the promise was vague.

## How to keep optimizing

- **Test the wording** of the invitation (for example "when we have a solution" versus "when early access opens") and compare opt-in rates.
- **Shorten the time gap.** Lists go cold. Contact people while the problem is still fresh, even just with a progress note.
- **Segment deeper.** Keep adding tags from later interactions (replied, interviewed, beta user).
- **Grow it beyond the survey.** Add the same pain-specific invitation to blog posts and community profiles as a low-commitment CTA.
- **Clean regularly.** Remove addresses that bounce and respect unsubscribes promptly.

## Common mistakes

- Making the email field mandatory. It cuts responses and pollutes the list with fake addresses.
- Throwing away the link between each email and that person's answers.
- Waiting months before the first contact.
- Sending generic newsletters instead of solution news tied to their problem.
- Promising something vague ("updates") and then emailing about unrelated things.

## Checklist

- [ ] Optional email field at the end of the survey
- [ ] Honest, specific promise in the invitation
- [ ] Emails stored with problem theme and difficulty
- [ ] Consent recorded and opt-out available
- [ ] First contact planned soon after the survey
- [ ] Launch emails written in the customers' words

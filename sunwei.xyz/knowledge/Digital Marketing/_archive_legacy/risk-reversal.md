# Risk Reversal: Make It Easy to Say Yes

## What it is

At the moment of decision, people hesitate because saying yes feels risky. The risk is not only money but also **time and effort**. **Minimizing the risk** is the course's fifth CTA tip: reduce the reader's fears and make it easy for them to say yes. The course shows three families of risk reducers:

1. **Money-back guarantees**
   - General: "Join today and enjoy our 100% money-back guarantee for 30 days."
   - Specific, results-based: "If you don't get X results by X time, I'll give you 100% of your investment back, no questions asked."
   The course notes the wording choices: "**investment**" frames the purchase as an investment rather than an expense, and "**no questions asked**" removes the fear of having to fight support for a refund.
2. **No-commitment trials:** "Start your free trial today. **No credit card required. No software to install.**" There's no card to cancel later and no heavy setup, so neither money nor time is at risk.
3. **Specific, small time commitments:** "Sign up in just 60 seconds", "get approved in just 8 minutes". Concrete short times tell the reader this won't take the next two hours.

In general marketing this is called **risk reversal**: shift the risk from the buyer to the seller, or remove it.

## Why it matters

Many interested prospects don't act. Not because they don't want the outcome, but because they fear wasting money, time or effort, or being stuck. Risk reversal removes those reasons at the exact point of the [[call-to-action|CTA]]. It also balances [[urgency-in-copy|urgency]]: urgency says "act now", and risk reversal says "acting now is safe". Use it wherever a new, unknown product asks for money, data, installation or time.

## How to use it

**Inputs:** a list of the reader's hesitations (from research, objections in comments, drop-off points) and what you can actually offer. **Output:** risk-reducing lines placed right next to the CTA, all of them true.

1. **List the risks the reader feels.** Money: "what if it doesn't work?" Time: "setup will eat my evening." Effort: "I'll have to migrate everything." Lock-in: "can I leave?" Data: "what happens to my files?" Use your research and real objections ([[customer-pain-points-and-desires|Pain Points and Desires]]).
2. **Match each risk to a reducer you can honestly offer:** guarantee → money risk. Free trial or no card → money and cancellation risk. "No install" or "under 2 minutes" → time risk. "Your files stay yours" → lock-in and data risk.
3. **Write it specifically.** Specific numbers and plain promises ("60 seconds", "no questions asked") are more believable than vague reassurance.
4. **Place it right under or beside the CTA,** where the hesitation happens.
5. **Make the process match the promise.** If you say "no questions asked", the refund really has to be one click or one email. If you say "60 seconds", time it yourself.
6. **Check your terms** for any guarantee you offer (payment provider rules, local consumer rules). That is general diligence, not course content.

> [!example] Illustration: MDFriday
> Likely hesitations for an Obsidian publishing plugin: "Will it mess up my vault?", "Is setup long?", "Am I locked in?" Honest reducers could be: "Your notes stay as plain Markdown files in your vault", "Publish your first folder in a couple of minutes" (only if timed and true), and, if a free tier or trial exists, "No credit card required". Each line must be checked against the real product before use.

## How to verify it worked

- **CTA conversion rate before and after** adding the risk-reducing line, holding everything else constant. An A/B test is best.
- **Drop-off at the decision step:** in sign-up or checkout flows, see whether fewer people abandon at the step where you added the reassurance.
- **Objection frequency:** track how often people ask about the risk you addressed (in comments, emails, calls). It should fall.
- **Refund or cancellation rate** after adding a guarantee or trial. A small rise can be fine if conversions rise more. A big rise may mean the offer attracts poor-fit buyers, or the product under-delivers.
- **Promise-keeping audit:** actually test the refund, cancellation and "60 seconds" claims yourself.

**Good:** more people take the action without a matching rise in regret (refunds, instant churn). **Bad:** conversions unchanged, which usually means you addressed a risk people don't actually feel. Go back to research.

## How to keep optimizing

- **Target the top objection first,** then the next one.
- **Test guarantee styles:** general time-based vs specific results-based.
- **Remove real friction,** not just the fear of it. Shortening setup is better than promising it's short.
- **Update reducers as the product changes,** such as new import options or easier cancellation.
- **Add social proof** from people who took the leap. It lowers perceived risk further ([[aida-formula|AIDA]]'s desire stage).

## Common mistakes

- Promising guarantees you won't honor, or hiding conditions in fine print.
- Addressing a risk nobody worried about while ignoring the real one.
- Vague reassurance ("risk-free!") with no specifics.
- Placing reassurance far from the CTA, where no one sees it at decision time.
- Claiming "no install" or "60 seconds" without checking it's true.

## Checklist

- [ ] Reader's real hesitations listed from research or objections
- [ ] Each covered by a true, specific reducer
- [ ] Reducer sits right next to the CTA
- [ ] Refund, cancellation or time claims tested personally
- [ ] Conversion and objection frequency compared before and after
- [ ] Refund or churn watched after adding guarantees

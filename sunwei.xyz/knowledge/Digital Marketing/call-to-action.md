# Writing an Irresistible Call to Action

## What it is

A **call to action (CTA)** is the explicit instruction telling the reader what to do next: start a trial, subscribe, download, reply, share. The course states: "a strong call to action is the difference between a page that converts and one that falls flat." It is the **action** stage of [[aida-formula|AIDA]].

The course gives five CTA tips. This article covers the three about clarity and presence. The other two have their own articles:

1. **A CTA on every page,** not just the homepage. Blog posts, emails and social posts all need one. The CTA doesn't always have to be the big one: it can be joining the list, replying to an email, or sharing. **Begin with the end in mind**: decide what action you want before you write anything.
2. **Start with a verb:** start, try, download, subscribe, watch, share, fill out, find out, shop, order. Low-commitment verbs like **start** or **try** feel less binding than **buy** or **shop**. The course cites Udemy's "try any course" as an example.
3. **Create urgency:** see [[urgency-in-copy|Honest Urgency]].
4. **Make it visible:** draw the eye with contrasting colors (a highlighted box in a blog post, a contrasting sidebar sign-up form), use bold text in emails, and in emails or social posts **paste the full link** instead of a buried "click here", because people recognize a link as something to click.
5. **Minimize the risk:** see [[risk-reversal|Risk Reversal]].

## Why it matters

People are busy. As the course puts it, they don't have time to work out what they should do next. Without a clear CTA, even persuasive content ends in nothing. Every piece is created for a reason, and the CTA is where that reason becomes a result. A CTA in every piece also builds a habit where you offer something and the audience responds, which builds the relationship over time.

## How to use it

**Inputs:** the goal of the piece and the reader's stage (new visitor, subscriber, trial user). **Output:** one primary CTA, placed and styled so it's impossible to miss.

1. **Decide the one action before writing.** Match it to the reader's stage: a cold reader might join a list, a warm one might start a trial, and a customer might reply or share.
2. **Write the CTA text starting with a verb,** plus the outcome: "Start publishing your notes", "Download the checklist", "Reply and tell me your biggest problem".
3. **Prefer low-commitment verbs** (try, start, get, see) when the ask is bigger than the reader's current trust.
4. **Make it visible:** use a contrasting button or box on web pages, bold or a full link on its own line in email, and a full link in social posts. Place it where the reader naturally finishes (end of post) and, on longer pages, near the top as well.
5. **Remove competing asks.** One primary CTA per piece. Secondary links shouldn't look like the main one.
6. **Add urgency and risk reduction** only where they're genuine ([[urgency-in-copy|Honest Urgency]], [[risk-reversal|Risk Reversal]]).
7. **Test it.** Click it yourself on mobile and desktop and make sure it works.

## How to verify it worked

- **CTA click-through rate:** clicks on the CTA divided by people who saw the page or email. Most email tools and web analytics can track this. Use tagged links to tell CTAs apart.
- **Completion rate:** of those who clicked, how many finished the action (signed up, installed, replied)? A big drop means friction after the click.
- **Visibility check:** watch a few people use the page, or use a heatmap or click-map tool, and see whether eyes and clicks reach the CTA.
- **Coverage audit:** go through your pages, posts and emails and count how many lack a CTA. The course's standard is every one.

Compare with your own past versions. The course gives no benchmark numbers. **Good:** clicks rise after making the CTA more visible or specific. **Bad:** high page views with almost no CTA clicks, which usually means the CTA is hidden, vague or asks too much too soon.

## How to keep optimizing

- **Test the verb** ("Try" vs "Start" vs "Get") and the outcome words.
- **Test placement and contrast:** top vs bottom, button vs link, color.
- **Match the ask to trust.** If a big ask fails, test a smaller first step (list or reply before trial).
- **Check the post-click path.** A great CTA leading to a slow or confusing page wastes the click.
- **Review old content.** Add CTAs to posts that have none.

## Common mistakes

- No CTA at all, or a vague "Learn more" with no clear outcome.
- Several equally loud CTAs competing for attention.
- A CTA the same color and style as everything else.
- "Click here" buried in a paragraph of an email.
- Asking for a purchase from a reader who just met you.

## Checklist

- [ ] Action decided before writing
- [ ] One primary CTA per piece
- [ ] Starts with a verb, names the outcome
- [ ] Low-commitment wording where trust is low
- [ ] Visually contrasting, or a full link in email and social
- [ ] Click-through and completion tracked

# Pain Points and Desires: The Foundation of Marketing

## What it is

A **pain point** is a problem the customer feels right now: something frustrating, slow, expensive, confusing or risky. A **desire** is the outcome they want instead: where they want to be and why it matters to them, both emotionally and logically.

The course says it plainly and repeats it in two sections: *there is nothing more powerful in marketing than intimately understanding a customer's pain points and desires.* The model behind it has three parts:

- **Where they are now** (the pain)
- **Where they want to be** (the desire, the ideal outcome)
- **Your product or service**, which is the bridge from one to the other

Once you know the first two, the course says, "all we have to do is create the product or the service that'll take them from where they are now to where they want to be." The customer's language about both ends becomes your marketing copy.

## Why it matters

It is step one, before any blog post or ad. The course describes people who complain that their Facebook ads didn't work. When the instructor looks, the ads address no pain point and no desire. Understanding pain and desire lets you:

- **Choose what to build.** Every product or service must first solve a problem the customer has.
- **Write copy that connects.** You can use headlines, bullets and first paragraphs that repeat what the customer already feels (see [[write-like-your-customers-talk|Write Like Your Customers Talk]]).
- **Save time and money.** You avoid building and promoting things nobody asked for.

Use it when you start something new, when a message isn't landing, or when you need to choose between feature ideas.

## How to use it

**Inputs:** access to potential customers (a community, a list, a forum, reviews). **Output:** a pain-and-desire map.

1. **Collect raw statements.** Use one or more of the [[four-customer-research-methods|four research methods]]: surveys, social media and communities, review sites, interviews. The fastest structured option is the [[three-question-market-research-survey|three-question survey]].
2. **Record exact words.** Copy what people actually wrote or said, not your summary of it.
3. **Separate pain from desire.** Tag each statement: *pain* ("I spend hours fixing broken links after every export") or *desire* ("I just want my notes online without thinking about it").
4. **Cluster.** Group statements into themes and count how often each theme comes up.
5. **Rate difficulty.** For each theme, note how hard people say it has been to solve (see [[unsolved-problem-filter|The Unsolved Problem Filter]]). Hard, frequent problems are the best targets.
6. **Write the bridge.** For the top theme, write one sentence: "People who [pain] want [desire]. We get them there by [how]."

A simple map template:

| Theme | Pain quotes | Desire quotes | Frequency | Difficulty |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ... | ... | ... | ... | ... |

> Hypothetical pain quote from an Obsidian user: "Every static site generator wants me to restructure my vault." Desire quote: "I want what I see in Obsidian to be what the site shows." Bridge: "Obsidian users who don't want to maintain a separate site workflow want to publish a folder as-is. A right-click publish gets them there." The quotes here are invented to show the format. Real ones must come from real users.

## How to verify it worked

- **Evidence test:** each top theme has several independent quotes from different people. One loud person is not a theme.
- **Recognition test:** read your top pain statement back to a target user. The signal to look for is something like "yes, exactly", or them adding their own details. A polite "I guess" means you're off.
- **Copy test:** put pain and desire language in a headline or post and compare it with a feature-led version. More replies, clicks or sign-ups on the pain-led version confirm the insight. Compare versions on the same channel and audience. Don't compare against outside benchmarks.
- **Behavior test:** when you offer a solution, people who reported the pain take the next step (join a list, install, reply). Words without action are a warning sign.

**Good:** themes repeat across different sources, and people react to your language. **Bad:** themes come from your own assumptions, or every theme appears only once.

## How to keep optimizing

- **Keep a living pain log.** Add every new statement from comments, support emails and conversations. Re-cluster monthly.
- **Go deeper with "why".** For a top pain, ask "why does that matter to you?" until you reach the emotional reason. The course's second survey question does this.
- **Track shifts.** When a new tool or platform change appears, check whether the pain has moved.
- **Test the language.** Keep swapping in fresh customer phrases in headlines and CTAs, and keep the ones that perform.
- **Narrow the audience.** If themes are scattered, you may be surveying too broad a group. Narrow the audience and repeat.

## Common mistakes

- Guessing the pain instead of collecting it.
- Writing down your paraphrase instead of the customer's words.
- Listing only pains and forgetting the desired outcome and why it matters.
- Chasing problems that are already easy to solve (no one will pay).
- Doing the research once and never updating it.

## Checklist

- [ ] At least one research method run with real people
- [ ] Exact quotes stored and tagged pain / desire
- [ ] Themes clustered and counted
- [ ] Difficulty noted for each theme
- [ ] A one-sentence bridge written for the top theme
- [ ] Language tested in at least one piece of copy

# Search-Friendly Titles: Keyword First, Right Length

## What it is

Two headline techniques from the course aimed at search engines like Google and YouTube:

1. **Keyword first, then punctuation.** Put the keyword you want to rank for at the start of the title, then add a colon or dash and a compelling promise. The course's example for the keyword "marketing plan template": *"Marketing Plan Template: Create Your First Marketing Plan in 5 Steps."* The course says this gives an SEO advantage because the keyword is at the start, and it makes it very clear to people what the post is about.
2. **Watch the length.** The course says Google typically shows the first 50–60 characters of a blog post title in search results. Anything longer may be cut off, so people won't see the whole title and may be less likely to click.

Together they give a title with two halves: a **search half** (the keyword, matching what people type) and a **human half** (the promise, which makes them choose you among the results).

## Why it matters

In search, your title competes on a results page with others for the same query. It has to show relevance (the keyword the searcher just typed) and appeal (a reason to click yours). A truncated title loses its promise. A clever title with no keyword may not match the search at all. Use this for blog posts, documentation pages, landing pages and YouTube videos you want found through search.

A general note, not from the course: search engines truncate titles based on display width rather than an exact character count, and they sometimes rewrite titles. Treat the course's 50–60 characters as a practical guideline, not a hard rule.

## How to use it

**Inputs:** the target keyword or phrase (what your audience actually types), the piece's main promise, and a character counter. **Output:** a title under the length guideline, with the keyword up front.

1. **Pick the keyword** from customer language and search suggestions: the phrase a real person would type, such as "publish obsidian notes as website".
2. **Put it first,** in natural form: "Publish Obsidian Notes as a Website".
3. **Add punctuation and a promise:** a colon or dash, then a benefit, number or specific outcome from [[magnetic-headlines|Magnetic Headlines]]: "...: A 2-Minute Folder Setup".
4. **Count the characters.** Aim to keep the whole title within about 50–60 characters, or at least make sure the keyword and the core promise fit in that range.
5. **Preview it.** Use a search-result preview tool, or simply read the first 55 characters alone. Does it still make sense?
6. **Keep the page consistent:** the body, especially the opening, should clearly cover the keyword topic so the title's promise is kept.

> Keyword: "obsidian publish folder". Title: "Obsidian Publish Folder: Turn Notes into a Site in 1 Click". That's about 58 characters, keyword first, promise second. For a YouTube video: "Obsidian to Website: Publish a Folder with One Right-Click". Illustrative. Check that each claim matches the real product.

## How to verify it worked

- **Search impressions and position:** in a search analytics tool (for example Google Search Console for sites, YouTube Studio for videos), check how often the page appears for the target keyword and at what position.
- **Click-through rate from search:** clicks divided by impressions for that query. This measures the title's human half. Compare before and after a title change over similar periods.
- **Truncation check:** search for your page and look at how the title actually shows. If the promise is cut off or the title was rewritten, shorten or clarify it.
- **Query match:** check which queries actually bring visitors. If they differ from your target keyword, either the keyword or the content needs adjusting.

**Good:** growing impressions for the target query and a click-through rate that rises after title edits. **Bad:** impressions but few clicks (weak promise or truncation), or no impressions for the target query (wrong keyword or content mismatch).

## How to keep optimizing

- **Review titles regularly** for pages with many impressions and low click-through. These are the quickest wins.
- **Test the promise half** while keeping the keyword stable, and change one thing at a time.
- **Follow real queries.** If people find the page with a slightly different phrase, consider using that phrase.
- **Refresh dates and numbers** in titles when the content is updated.
- **Don't churn titles too often.** Give each change enough time and data before judging it.

## Common mistakes

- Stuffing several keywords into one title.
- Putting the keyword at the end, where it may be cut off.
- Titles so long the promise never appears in results.
- A keyword that nobody searches for, or phrased in your jargon instead of the customer's.
- Changing the title without updating the content to match.

## Checklist

- [ ] Keyword chosen from real customer or search language
- [ ] Keyword at the start of the title
- [ ] Punctuation separates keyword and promise
- [ ] Keyword and promise fit within about 50–60 characters
- [ ] Appearance in search results checked
- [ ] Search click-through rate reviewed after changes

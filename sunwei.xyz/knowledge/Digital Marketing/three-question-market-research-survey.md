# The Three-Question Market Research Survey

## What it is

A short survey, built for free in Google Forms in the course, that uses three "magic" strategic questions and an optional email field. Each part has one job:

1. **Problem (short answer):** *"What is your biggest problem with [your topic]?"* This finds the pain points.
2. **Outcome (long answer):** *"Why would it make a difference in your life to get a good answer for this problem or find a solution for your need? (Details, please.)"* This finds the ideal outcome and its emotional and logical benefits.
3. **Difficulty (multiple choice):** *"How difficult has it been for you to find a good answer for the above to date?"* with the options Not at all difficult / Somewhat difficult / Very difficult. This filters for problems that aren't well solved in the market.
4. **Bonus, email (short answer):** *"Add your email below if you'd like to be contacted when we have a solution to your problem."* This builds a list before you have a product.

The course promises three results from this one process: a list of things people need and will pay for, ready-made sales copy in the customers' own words, and an early email list.

## Why it matters

The questions look simple, but they are strategic. Question 1 names the problem. Question 2 describes the destination and why it matters. Question 3 tells you whether a market gap exists. If people already have an easy solution, the course warns, "we're not gonna be able to sell anything." Use this survey:

- before you build a product or write your first blog post
- when you're choosing between product ideas
- when your copy isn't connecting and you need fresh customer language

## How to use it

**Inputs:** a topic or niche, a free form tool (the course uses Google Forms for its simplicity, built-in analytics and unlimited free responses), and somewhere to share it.

1. **Create the form.** Name it after your topic or product so people know what it's about (for example, "Digital Marketing Survey").
2. **Add Question 1** as a short-answer field and fill in your niche: "What is your biggest problem with *[static publishing from Obsidian]*?"
3. **Add Question 2** as a long-answer (paragraph) field. Keep "Details, please." It invites longer, richer answers.
4. **Add Question 3** as multiple choice with the three difficulty options.
5. **Add the optional email field** and make it clearly optional.
6. **Delete the default placeholder question** and check spelling. A sloppy form lowers trust.
7. **Test it yourself** and with one friend, then share it where your audience already is (course lecture 9 covers distribution, but its transcript is empty in this copy; see the note in Source).
8. **Export the answers** to a spreadsheet and analyze them with [[unsolved-problem-filter|The Unsolved Problem Filter]].

**Outputs:** raw pain statements, outcome statements, a difficulty rating for each, and a list of opted-in emails.

## How to verify it worked

- **Response quality:** Q2 answers should be full sentences with reasons and feelings. One-word answers mean the question was framed poorly or you reached the wrong audience.
- **Theme concentration:** after clustering Q1, a few themes should cover a large share of answers. If everything is scattered, the audience is too broad.
- **Gap signal:** check how many answers to Q1 are rated "very difficult" in Q3. Those are the candidate opportunities. If nearly everything is "not at all difficult", the problem space is well served.
- **Opt-in rate:** the share of respondents who leave an email shows how much they want a solution. Track it over time and between audiences. Don't judge it against outside numbers.
- **Copy yield:** count how many phrases you'd be willing to reuse word for word in a headline or email.

## How to keep optimizing

- **Sharpen the topic word.** "Marketing" gives vague answers. "Getting first customers for a SaaS" gives sharp ones. Test narrower versions.
- **Compare audiences.** Run the same form in two communities and compare themes and difficulty.
- **Follow up.** Email people who answered "very difficult" and ask for a short conversation (see [[four-customer-research-methods|Four Ways to Uncover Pain Points and Desires]]).
- **Re-run on a schedule** (for example quarterly, or after a big product change) to catch shifts.
- **Keep the form short.** Every extra question costs responses. Add questions only if you'll act on the answers.

## Common mistakes

- Adding a dozen extra questions and killing the completion rate.
- Asking leading questions ("Would you use a tool that...?") instead of open problem questions.
- Skipping Question 3 and chasing problems that are already solved.
- Making the email field mandatory, which scares people off and biases the sample.
- Summarizing answers instead of keeping the exact wording for copy.

## Checklist

- [ ] Form named clearly after the topic
- [ ] Q1 problem, Q2 outcome ("Details, please."), Q3 difficulty
- [ ] Optional email field
- [ ] Placeholder question removed, spelling checked
- [ ] Shared where the target audience already is
- [ ] Answers exported and filtered by difficulty

# The Unsolved Problem Filter

## What it is

A painful problem is not enough. If people already have an easy, good-enough solution, they won't pay for yours. The **unsolved problem filter** is the third question of the [[three-question-market-research-survey|three-question survey]], used as a screen:

> "How difficult has it been for you to find a good answer for the above to date?"
> Not at all difficult / Somewhat difficult / Very difficult

The course calls it a filtering question. Its purpose is to "identify problems that are not well solved in the marketplace". In the course's words: "If they have a problem and they have a solution and it's not at all difficult to find that solution, well then we're not gonna be able to sell anything."

The filter crosses two things:

- **What** the problem is (from Question 1) and why it matters (Question 2)
- **How well the market already solves it** (Question 3)

Problems that are both common and "very difficult" are where a new product can win.

## Why it matters

Founders usually fall in love with a problem. This filter checks whether the market agrees that the problem is still open. Use it when you:

- choose which problem your product or next feature should target
- decide which pain to lead with in your headline and landing page
- check whether a niche is too crowded with good solutions

It is also a defence against fooling yourself. You judge an idea by the customer's struggle, not by your own excitement.

## How to use it

**Inputs:** exported survey answers (problem text, outcome text, difficulty rating). **Output:** a ranked shortlist of unsolved problems.

1. **Export** the answers to a spreadsheet, one row per respondent.
2. **Code Question 1.** Read each problem and give it a theme label (for example "setup is confusing", "themes look bad", "links break"). Keep the original text next to it.
3. **Count per theme,** split by difficulty: how many said very, somewhat or not at all difficult.
4. **Rank the themes.** Put themes with many "very difficult" answers at the top. Put themes dominated by "not at all difficult" at the bottom. Those problems are already solved for most people.
5. **Read the Question 2 answers** for your top themes. The strongest opportunities have both high difficulty and strong outcome language ("it would save my weekends", "I could finally ship my portfolio").
6. **Shortlist one to three themes** and write down, for each: the pain, the desired outcome, why existing options fail (if people said so), and quotes to reuse.
7. **Check for yourself.** For each shortlisted theme, look at the existing solutions and their reviews to understand why people still struggle (see [[four-customer-research-methods|Four Ways to Uncover Pain Points and Desires]]).

> Imagine two themes from a survey of Obsidian users: "I want a nicer theme for my existing site" (mostly "not at all difficult") and "I can't publish without learning a static site generator" (mostly "very difficult"). The filter says: lead with the second. The numbers here are hypothetical. The method is the point.

## How to verify it worked

The filter works if the problems it selects turn into real demand:

- **Follow-up conversations:** people who said "very difficult" should be keen to talk. Invite a handful and see how many say yes.
- **Opt-in rate by difficulty:** compare how often "very difficult" and "not at all difficult" respondents left their email. If the filter is picking real gaps, the "very difficult" group should opt in more often.
- **Message response:** a post or landing page that leads with the top unsolved problem should get more replies, clicks or sign-ups than one that leads with a low-difficulty problem. Test on the same channel.
- **Conversion later:** once you have something to offer, check whether people from the top theme actually try or buy.

**Good:** a clear leader among the themes, with high difficulty and emotional outcome language. **Bad:** difficulty ratings spread evenly across all themes, which usually means the audience is too broad or the topic word too vague.

## How to keep optimizing

- **Increase the sample** before making a big decision. Small samples can put a theme on top by chance.
- **Segment.** Split results by audience source (which community or channel) to see whether the gaps differ between groups.
- **Add a follow-up question** only when needed, for example "What have you tried so far?" to learn why current options fail.
- **Re-run after launches.** When you or a competitor ships a solution, the difficulty rating for that theme should fall. If it doesn't, the solution isn't landing.
- **Watch for "somewhat difficult" clusters.** They can mean a partial solution exists and a better-positioned one could win.

## Common mistakes

- Ignoring Question 3 and ranking only by how common a problem is.
- Treating one dramatic answer as a trend.
- Losing the original wording while coding themes.
- Assuming "very difficult" means "will pay". It is a strong signal, not proof. Confirm it with behavior.
- Mixing very different audiences in one analysis.

## Checklist

- [ ] Answers exported and coded into themes
- [ ] Themes counted by difficulty level
- [ ] Top themes ranked by "very difficult" count
- [ ] Outcome language read for top themes
- [ ] Existing solutions checked for why they fail
- [ ] Shortlist confirmed with follow-up conversations or opt-ins

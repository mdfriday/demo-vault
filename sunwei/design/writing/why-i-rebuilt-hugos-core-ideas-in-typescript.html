Why I Rebuilt Hugo's Core Ideas in TypeScript

How studying a mature static site generator shaped MDFriday's engine — and why “rebuild” meant learning the ideas, not cloning the codebase.

Technology · Dec 3, 2025 · 12 min read

After reading Hugo's source, I had a clearer map of publishing systems. The next question was practical: what does MDFriday need that a classic static generator doesn't fully cover?

People already write in Obsidian. They already have graphs, folders, and habits. The product problem wasn't “another SSG.” It was: how do you publish what you already know without rebuilding your workflow?

Ideas worth keeping

  • Content as the source of truth
  • A deterministic build pipeline
  • Templates as the presentation boundary
  • Fast feedback when something changes

What had to change

TypeScript put the engine closer to the rest of the product: UI, AI helpers, and browser tooling. It also forced clearer types around content models — pages, notes, gardens, and publish targets.

I wasn't trying to replace Hugo for everyone. I was trying to own the intermediate representation that MDFriday needs: from personal knowledge to a public site people can maintain.

Don't copy a system. Extract the decisions that made it work.

Still learning

The engine is part of an experiment, not a finished proof. Some choices will change after talking to users. That's the point of building in public: the architecture has to survive contact with real problems.
